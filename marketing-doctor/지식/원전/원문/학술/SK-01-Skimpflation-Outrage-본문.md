## Title page

Marketing Science Institute Working Paper Series 2024
Report No. 24-107

Skimpflation Outrage: Decreases in Product Quality are Seen as Much More Unfair Than
Decreases in Size and Increases in Price

Ioannis Evangelidis
ESADE, Universitat Ramon Llull
Date of Submission: February 27, 2024.

## Introduction

In recent years, manufacturers have repeatedly encountered increases in their costs.
Historically, their strategy to counteract these rising costs involved increasing the prices of their
products. Raising prices is a viable response to cost increases because consumers typically accept
price hikes as a legitimate measure for firms to protect their profits (Kahneman, Knetsch, and
Thaler 1986). […]

Nonetheless, an alternative response to cost increases has also emerged and gained
considerable traction during the past years. That response, referred to as "shrinkflation," involves
decreasing the size of the product without changing its price. […] Interestingly, Evangelidis
(2023) found that consumers particularly disapprove of shrinkflation, viewing it as deceptive
because it's harder to detect changes in size compared to price. As a result, in the studies of
Evangelidis (2023), the proportion of respondents who judged product downsizing as an unfair
practice was much higher than the proportion who judged equivalent price increases as unfair.
These findings suggest that there may be a need for policy-makers to protect consumers against
shrinkflation. To this end, France and Germany are considering imposing bans on shrinkflation
(Neerman 2023).

Interestingly, besides shrinkflation, a third type of response to cost increases has emerged
and is becoming exceedingly common in recent times. That response, referred to as
"skimpflation," centers around decreasing the quality of the product without changing the
product's size or price. For example, Sainsbury's reduced the proportion of olive oil in its olive
spread from 21% to 10%, M&S replaced red onions with spring onions in their chips, while Aldi
decreased the amout of egg yolk in its mayo from 9% to 7.5% (Prosser 2023). […]

[…] a series of preregistered studies across a wide range of product categories suggest that
consumers find reductions in product quality significantly more unfair than reductions in product
size or price increases. In fact, the vast majority of respondents in my studies judge skimpflation
as unfair, which is not always the case for shrinkflation. I label this phenomenon "skimpflation
outrage."

## GENERAL DISCUSSION

[…] Through six preregistered studies spanning a variety of product categories, I found that
consumers judge skimpflation as an extremely unfair practice, a phenomenon which I labeled
"skimpflation outrage." Remarkably, across all studies and product categories, reductions in
product quality were consistently deemed significantly more unfair than either reductions in
product size or price increases. Notably, the overwhelming majority of participants in my studies
judged skimpflation to be unfair, a sentiment not as commonly echoed for shrinkflation. In sharp
contrast, price increases were typically judged to be fair by most consumers, attributed to the
belief that price increases are less deceptive, being more noticeable than changes in quality or
size. Interestingly, consumers found quality and size reductions equally difficult to detect. The
data indicate that the disparity in fairness judgments between these two forms of reductions stems
from the fact that quality decreases directly alter the product and, consequently, the consumption
experience. […]

### Theoretical Contributions (발췌)

The foundational study by Kahneman et al. (1986) explored how consumers perceive fairness in
price increases, concluding that such increases are deemed fair when they stem from surges in
costs threatening a company's baseline (i.e., reference) profits. Nonetheless, Bolton, Warlop, and
Alba (2003) report that consumers may often underestimate the fair price of products because
they may neglect considering the full range of firm costs, as well as inflation rates. Interventions
making those factors salient can boost judgments of fairness. Moreover, building on Kahneman
et al. (1986), Bolton and Alba (2006) report that consumers may judge price increases differently
depending on the extent to which the costs are alignable to the changes in price. Certain cost
categories (e.g., production costs) are considered to be more alignable with changes in price
compared with others (e.g., general overhead costs), which can lead to consumers judging an
increase in price as more fair when an alignable cost is involved.

### Implications for Firms and Policy-Makers (발췌)

For businesses, especially those grappling with rising costs, my findings clearly indicate that
decreasing the quality of products is not a viable option. Consumers strongly prefer price
increases over reductions in quality or size. From this author's viewpoint, it is crucial for
companies to be mindful of consumer perceptions regarding the fairness of their actions.
Reducing product quality or size may offer short-term financial gains, but it's likely to incur
long-term reputational damage, undermining the company's image and consumer trust.
