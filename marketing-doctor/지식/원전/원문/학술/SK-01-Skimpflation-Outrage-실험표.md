## STUDY 1A

**Procedure**

Participants (N = 200; Prolific) were asked to judge a chocolate manufacturer's decision
to increase the price, to decrease the size, and to decrease the quality of its product in response to
cost increases. The order of the three questions was randomized. Participants judged whether the
change was fair versus unfair:

Study 1a (N = 200)

Price Increase
> Production costs for a chocolate manufacturer recently increased. As a result, the
> firm decided to increase the price of its product. The size and quality of the product
> will remain the same. This is
> -fair [85%]
> -unfair [15%]

Size Decrease
> Production costs for a chocolate manufacturer recently increased. As a result, the
> firm decided to decrease the size of its product. The price and quality of the product
> will remain the same. This is
> -fair [57.5%]
> -unfair [42.5%]

Quality Decrease
> Production costs for a chocolate manufacturer recently increased. As a result, the
> firm decided to decrease the quality of its product. The price and size of the product
> will remain the same. This is
> -fair [16.5%]
> -unfair [83.5%]

**Results**

The data were consistent with the phenomenon of "skimpflation outrage": the proportion
of participants who judged decreasing the product's quality as unfair was much higher than the
proportion who judged decreasing the product's size as unfair (83.5% vs. 42.5%, z = 8.373, p <
.001), and the proportion who judged increasing the product's price as unfair (83.5% vs. 15%, z =
11.919, p < .001). Note that, consistent with Evangelidis (2023), the proportion of participants
who judged decreasing the product's size as unfair was higher than the proportion who judged
increasing the product's price as unfair (42.5% vs. 15%, z = 7.124, p < .001).

This is [Fair: 17.7%, Unfair: 82.3%]

In conclusion, data of Study 5 shed light unto why skimpflation is judged to be more
unfair than shrinkflation. While both practices are seen as less transparent compared with raising
prices, skimpflation is perceived to be even more unfair than shrinkflation because it involves
more profound product changes. That is, reducing the product's quality directly alters the product
itself and, by extension, the consumption experience. This contrasts with merely adjusting the
product's size, which may restrict consumption volume but does not affect the intrinsic quality or
sensory experience of the product.
